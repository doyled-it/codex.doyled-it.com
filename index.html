Ofnir

2015 / 2018 · debut

Heilung's debut — here in full, all nine tracks in album order. (The valkyrie name-list once placed here as "Krigsgaldr" was in fact Svanrand from Futha, now in its correct home; the real Krigsgaldr — the Eggja-stone war-chant — sits at track 2.)

ᛟ

Alfaðirhaiti

"The by-names of the All-Father." A heiti is a poetic alternative name — and this song is a litany of Óðinn's many names and sacred attributes, sealed with an archaic Germanic consecration chant.

⚜ Sources behind the names

  • Odin's by-names (heiti) are recited in the Eddic Grímnismál, where the god names himself again and again.
  • Catalogue of the names & their senses: List of names of Odin.
  • The treasures & beasts: Draupnir, Geri & Freki, Sleipnir, Gungnir.
  • The refrain is the Migration-era runic inscription on the Kragehul I spear-shaft (Denmark, DR 196, c. 5th–6th c., Elder Futhark) — see reading below.
  • The "gaegogae" element parallels the Undley bracteate's gægogæ; on the wiju "I dedicate/consecrate" formula see runic-amulet scholarship.
Original — treasures & beasts
Draupnir Geri, Freki Sleipnir Gungnir
Rendered — meaning
Draupnir — the gold ring that drips eight new rings every ninth night. Geri & Freki — "the greedy" & "the ravenous," Odin's two wolves. Sleipnir — his eight-legged horse. Gungnir — his spear that never misses.
Refrain · Proto-Germanic ·
Haegolae Haegolae Haegolae Wiju Bi Gojze Gaegogae Gaegogae Gaegogae Ginu Gahelija
Rendered — the Kragehul I reading
This is the inscription on the Kragehul I spear-shaft, not Old Norse. The scholarly reading (MacLeod & Mees, Runic Amulets and Magic Objects, 2006) of the original — gagaga ginu gahellija, hagala wiju bi g[aize] — runs roughly: "gagaga, I yell resoundingly; hail I dedicate in[to] the spear." A battle-magic consecration: wiju = "I consecrate/dedicate," hagala = "hail," gagaga a war-cry. Earlier editors read it differently (Schneider, Düwel, Pieper).1
Original — Óðinn's heiti (Grímnismál)
Fimbulþulr, Fjǫlnir · Uðr, Ulfrǫgni Þekkr, Þudu · Óski, Ófnir Rǫgnir, Rauðir · Grímnir, Gǫndlir Hléfreyr, Hangatýr · Njólstapi, Náðvindr Jǫlfuðr, Jafnhár · Atriðir, Alfaðir Síðgrani, Sigfǫðr · Dughirgjafi, Dresvarpr Bileygur, Biflindi · Margvisir, Miðvitnis Lǫndungr, Launhirdir · Yggr ok Ýjungr
Rendered — a sampling of the by-names
Name after name, each a facet of the god: Fimbulþulr "mighty sage/poet"; Fjǫlnir "concealer / many-shaped"; Óski "wish-giver"; Ófnir / Grímnir "the masked one"; Hangatýr "god of the hanged"; Jafnhár "even-as-high"; Alfaðir "All-Father"; Sigfǫðr "father of victory"; Bileygr "the one with poor / wavering sight" (distinct from Báleygr "flame-eyed"); Biflindi "shield-shaker"; Atriðr "attacker / rider"; Yggr "the terrible one."2
Original — closing
Haugen Maunen Ok Alfaðir heitir
Rendered — English
The mound and the moon — and he is called All-Father.

¹ The refrain is the Elder-Futhark inscription on the Kragehul I spear-shaft (DR 196, Migration period). It is genuinely difficult: wiju ("I dedicate/consecrate") and hagala ("hail") are reasonably secure, but the gagaga/gaegogae war-cry and exact phrasing have several competing scholarly readings. Heilung's "Wiju Bi Gojze" preserves the wiju bi g[aize] "I dedicate in[to] the spear" of the MacLeod & Mees reading.

² The Old Norse heiti are attested in Grímnismál (which alone lists ~47–50 names in stanzas 46–50) and the þulur; meanings here follow the scholarly catalogue of Odin's names. Only a sampling is glossed, and a few spellings in the sung text are normalized or variant forms.

Krigsgaldr

"War-galdr / war-chant." Heilung's best-known song. Its sung chorus is the Eggja stone inscription — the same runic text as Hakkerskaldyr — set against a long original-English bridge: a meditation on being met only with "the language of the sword."

⚜ Sources

  • The chorus is the Eggja stone (N KJ101, c. 650–700 AD), in Ottar Grønvik's reading. Wikipedia notes directly that both this song and Hakkerskaldyr draw their lyrics from Grønvik's translation — see the Hakkerskaldyr chapter for the full runic breakdown.
  • The English bridge ("the language of the sword…") is original modern-English lyric by Kai Uwe Faust; text per Heilung's Bandcamp.
Chorus · Eggja stone · Grønvik ·
Min warb naseu, wilr made thaim i bormotha hauni. Hu war, hu war opkam har a hit lot? Got nafiskr orf, auim suimade, foki afa galande.
Rendered — Grønvik reading
The death-wave (or household's waning) was cast over them, with the weary mast-top… Who brought the host over to the land beyond? The fish of the gods, swimming out of the streams, the bird crowing in the drift of … (See Hakkerskaldyr for the contested runology — this is one scholarly reading of a famously difficult inscription.)1
Bridge — English (original Heilung)
What am I supposed to do if I want to talk about peace and understanding but you only understand the language of the sword? …I let the blade do the talking, so my tongue shall become iron and my words the mighty roar of war.
As written
Already in English. A lament at being unable to reach someone who answers only with violence — until the speaker resolves to "let the blade do the talking," their tongue turning to iron.2
Bridge — English (continued)
Beloved brother enemy, I sing my sword song for you, the lullaby of obliteration, so I can wake up with a smile and bliss in my heart. …Coexistence, conflict, combat; devastation, regeneration, transformation — that is the best I can do for you.
As written
The turn that gives the song its meaning: the enemy is also a "beloved brother," the violence a grim cleansing that the speaker hopes precedes renewal — a "powerful sun" rising to melt all moons and make "the old fires of purification look like dying embers."3

¹ The chorus is identical in source to Hakkerskaldyr: the Eggja stone, the longest Elder Futhark inscription, in Grønvik's (1985) reading. The runology is heavily contested (Olsen, Krause, Grønvik all differ); the fuller treatment, including the famous grave-ward "C" panel, is in the Hakkerskaldyr chapter.

² Unlike most Heilung material, the bridge is not a historical text but original English by Kai Uwe Faust — which is why it is given here as written rather than "translated."

³ The recurring triads ("coexistence, conflict, combat / devastation, regeneration, transformation") frame war as part of a cycle of destruction and renewal — the "healing" (Heilung) that the band's very name points to. The song is widely read as anti-war despite its martial surface.

Hakkerskaldyr

Built almost entirely from the Eggja stone — a 7th-century Norwegian grave-slab bearing the longest known Elder Futhark inscription, and one of the most cryptic. Heilung uses the reading of runologist Ottar Grønvik (1985).

A note on certainty. The Eggja inscription is genuinely contested: many runes are faint or ambiguous, and scholars (Olsen 1919, Krause & Jankuhn 1966, Grønvik 1985, and others) disagree sharply on both reading and meaning. The rendering below follows Grønvik, whose transliteration Heilung's lyrics track most closely. Treat it as one scholarly interpretation, not a settled translation.

⚜ Sources

  • The Eggja stone (Rundata N KJ101), ploughed up at Eggja farm, Sogndal, Norway in 1917; dated c. 650–700 AD. Its own Wikipedia article names this Heilung song as a use of the inscription.
  • Reading & translation: Ottar Grønvik, Runene på Eggjasteinen (1985), as transcribed on the Eggja stone article.
  • Recent scholarship on the disputed lines: Fornvännen 2024 (Fridell).
Opening · Heilung frame ·
Y! Ylir men Ae! Aero Their Era Mela os
Rendered
An incantatory frame Heilung adds around the stone-text — chant-syllables and rune-names rather than part of the Eggja inscription itself. Not attested on the stone.1
Eggja — A1 (runic transliteration)
hin warp naseu wilʀ made þaim kaiba i bormoþa huni
Grønvik (1985)
The household wanes; *Vil cast a death-wave over those — the oarlocks wore out for them, with the weary mast-top. (A description of a shipwreck in foul weather.)
Eggja — A2
huwaʀ ob kam harie a hit lat
Grønvik (1985)
Who brought the host to the land beyond?
Eggja — A3
gotna fiskʀ oʀ firnauim suwimade foki af fanwanga lande
Grønvik (1985)
The fish of the gods (Gotnar), out of the streams of Firnøy, swimming in the drift from the land of shining meadows.2

¹ "Y! Ylir men / Ae! Aero Their / Era Mela os" is not on the Eggja stone — it reads as Heilung's own opening invocation (echoing rune-names like Yr and ár). The song's body is the stone; this is staging around it.

² Every part of this is uncertain. Earlier readings differ wildly — Magnus Olsen (1919) read it as an epitaph for a household-master treacherously killed, with a blood-soaked corpse drawn to the farm by sled. Grønvik (1985) reads a drowning/shipwreck and a journey of the dead to the otherworld. The stone's "C" panel (not in this song) carries the famous protective line: roughly "the stone is not touched by sun nor cut by sword; let no man uncover it…" — a grave-ward against disturbance.

Schlammschlacht

"Mud-battle." Like Afhomon, this is original modern German, not an assembled ancient text — a vivid narrative poem retelling a real event: the Battle of the Teutoburg Forest (9 AD), where Germanic tribes annihilated three Roman legions. The renderings below are a direct translation of Heilung's German; only the closing Latin is a historical quotation.

A note on this entry. The German body is Heilung's own composition, so what follows is translation, not philology. What is verifiable is the history it dramatizes — the commanders, the ambush, the three-day slaughter, the aftermath — and the Latin lament that closes it, which is sourced below.

⚜ Sources

  • The event: the Battle of the Teutoburg Forest, 9 AD — the Roman general Publius Quinctilius Varus ("Varus") versus the Cheruscan leader Arminius ("Armin"), who had been raised as a Roman hostage and lured Varus into ambush.
  • The closing line is Augustus's lament, recorded by Suetonius, Divus Augustus 23.1: "Quintili Vare, legiones redde!"
  • Three legions — roughly an eighth of Rome's army — were destroyed over three days; Augustus reportedly mourned for months.
German — the threatening wood
Bedrohlich, der germanische Wald, schon seit Urzeit finster und kalt, voller Tropfen, Geheimnisse und Lichter, verflochten, verwoben in tote Gesichter.
Rendered — English
Threatening, the Germanic forest, dark and cold since the dawn of time, full of droplets, secrets and lights, interwoven, interlaced into dead faces.
German — the marching legions
Es rinnt der Schweiß, es rinnt der Regen, alle Legionäre müssen alles geben. Bis zu den Knien im Schlamm sie waten, sich immer enger und enger scharen; tiefe Furcht sich in ihre Kehlen schnürt.
Rendered — English
Sweat runs, rain runs; every legionary must give his all. Knee-deep in the mud they wade, gathering ever tighter and tighter; deep dread tightens around their throats.
German — the two commanders
Doch: "Semper fidelis!" — immer treu — folgen sie Varus, ihrem Führer, den seinerseits Armin, der Cherusker, führt. Plötzlich… als der Cherusker einen Pfiff ertönen lässt, und die Legionen stachen in ein Wespennest.
Rendered — English
Yet "Semper fidelis!" — ever faithful — they follow Varus, their commander, who is in turn led by Armin (Arminius) the Cheruscan. Suddenly… the Cheruscan sounds a whistle, and the legions had stabbed into a wasps' nest.1
German — the ambush
Aus dem Schlamm, den Blättern auf Boden und Bäumen, plötzlich unendlich Germanen schäumen. Ein Schrei wie von tausend Bären ertönt… Pfeile und Speere von Bäumen und Hügeln regnen. Doch auch schwarze Gesichter gehen nach Walhall.
Rendered — English
From the mud, the leaves, the ground and the trees, suddenly endless Germani come foaming forth. A cry as of a thousand bears resounds… Arrows and spears rain from trees and hills. Yet soot-blackened faces too pass on to Valhalla.2
German — the carnage
Es regnet Köpfe und Arme und Hände, blutrot ist des Waldes Moor am Ende. Bis zur dritten Nacht zieht sich das Grauen; im Lichte der Fackeln sieht man die Frauen, wie sie berauben die Römer…
Rendered — English
It rains heads and arms and hands; blood-red is the forest's bog in the end. The horror drags on until the third night; by torchlight one sees the women plundering the Romans…
German — the aftermath
Nur die Römer bleiben, genagelt an Bäume, im Blutmoor zurück. Auf Altären geopfert, Stück für Stück, ihre Schädel und Waffen Wotan dargebracht im heiligen Haine, im Schutze der Nacht. Und weit in Walhalla droben noch lang die Cherusker sich lobten, wie brav sie die Römer erzogen!
Rendered — English
Only the Romans remain, nailed to trees, left in the blood-bog. Sacrificed on altars, piece by piece, their skulls and weapons offered to Wotan (Odin) in the sacred grove, under cover of night. And far up in Valhalla the Cheruscans long praised themselves — how nicely they had "schooled" the Romans!3
Closing · Latin · Suetonius ·
"Quintili Vare, legiones redde!"
Rendered — English
"Quinctilius Varus, give me back my legions!" — the emperor Augustus's cry of grief at the loss of three legions, recorded by Suetonius.4

¹ Arminius (German "Hermann/Armin") was a Cheruscan nobleman raised as a hostage in Rome, who served as a trusted auxiliary commander under Varus and used that trust to lead the legions into the ambush. The "Semper fidelis" ("always faithful") is the song's ironic Roman motto against that betrayal.

² Heilung lace the historical account with Norse-myth colour: fallen warriors "go to Valhalla," and "soot-blackened faces" are the Germani themselves. These are the band's poetic framing, not part of the ancient record.

³ Roman sources (Tacitus, Annals I.61) describe Germanicus's troops later finding the site strewn with bones, skulls nailed to trees, and altars where tribunes had been sacrificed — which this stanza closely echoes. "Wotan" is the Old High German form of Odin.

⁴ Suetonius, Divus Augustus 23.1: Augustus reportedly let his hair and beard grow for months, struck his head against the doorpost crying this out, and kept the battle's anniversary as a day of mourning.

Carpathian Forest

The one track on the album already in modern English — an original Heilung composition (Juul & Faust), not a historical text. A short, incantatory nature-vision of the Carpathian Mountains. There is nothing to translate; it is presented as written, with light notes on its imagery.

⚜ Sources

  • Original lyric by Heilung, from Ofnir (2015); text verified against the band's own Bandcamp lyrics (many sites carry garbled auto-transcriptions — this is the authoritative version).
  • No runic inscription or historical poem underlies it; it is atmospheric imagery of the Carpathian Mountains.
Original — English (as written)
Carpathian forest Where the trees are endless Forest titans fall to rest A bed is made for the queen Mushrooms gather in circles Where bears walk Dereliction houses dark spirits A comet falls deep into darkness Stones rise in circles Where drums roar Water whispers vibrating People dance under the moon In circles Bone to Rune Skull to Dust Ice melts the thoughts of the gods Root promises blossom
Notes on the imagery
A vision of the deep Carpathian woods as sacred ground: ancient trees ("forest titans") falling, fungi growing in fairy rings, bears abroad, and stone circles where people drum and dance under the moon — folk-ritual imagery rather than a specific myth. The closing turns transformative: "Bone to Rune, Skull to Dust… Root promises blossom" — death feeding renewal, the cycle that runs through much of Heilung's work.1

¹ "Werebears walk" appears in some printings; the band's own Bandcamp text reads "Where bears walk." Either way the bear imagery fits the wider Germanic and Carpathian reverence for the bear. As original English, this song needs no translation — its place in the codex is as the album's one piece written plainly in the listener's own tongue, a quiet counterpoint to the ancient inscriptions around it.

Fylgija Ear

The same death-stanza twice: a German paraphrase, then its source — the Ear (ᛠ) stanza of the Anglo-Saxon Rune Poem, the poem's final rune. Ear is the rune of the grave; a fylgja is a Norse fetch or accompanying spirit.

⚜ Sources

  • The Old English is the closing Ear stanza of the Anglo-Saxon Rune Poem (recorded by George Hickes, 1705, from a now-lost Cotton MS).
  • English translation is Bruce Dickins, Runic and Heroic Poems of the Old Teutonic Peoples (1915) — full text: Wikisource.
  • On the rune itself & its name: Wikipedia — Ear (rune); Dickins connects the name to Old Norse aurr ("loam, clay"), hence "ground / grave."
  • The German half is Heilung's own free parallel rendering of the same stanza.
Original — German (Heilung's paraphrase)
Grab bleibt Ekel jedem Edeling Wenn faulendes Fleisch sich gärt Grund klagt grausam kühlend Fahren die Gaben hin, verfaulen Garben Freude bricht fort, Verträge brechen
Rendered — English
The grave stays loathsome to every nobleman when rotting flesh ferments; the ground laments, cruelly cooling. The gifts pass away, the sheaves rot; joy breaks off, covenants break.
Original — Old English (Anglo-Saxon Rune Poem)
Ear byþ egle eorla gehwylcun, ðonne fæstlice flæsc onginneþ, hraw colian, hrusan ceosan blac to gebeddan; bleda gedreosaþ, wynna gewitaþ, wera geswicaþ.
Rendered — Dickins (1915)
The grave is horrible to every knight, when the corpse quickly begins to cool and is laid in the bosom of the dark earth. Prosperity declines, happiness passes away and covenants are broken.1

¹ Quoted from Bruce Dickins's 1915 edition. A more literal word-gloss runs: "Ear is grievous to every earl, when the flesh firmly begins — the corpse — to cool, to choose the earth, pale, as a bedfellow; fruits/glories fail, joys depart, pledges (men) cease." The rune-name Ear appears only in runic alphabets; Dickins links it to ON aurr "loam/clay," i.e. the grave-earth — which is why this closing rune is read as death itself.

² The two halves are the same text: Heilung's German is a free parallel, with "Verträge brechen" mirroring Dickins's "covenants are broken" and "Freude bricht fort" his "happiness passes away."

Futhorck

The complete Anglo-Saxon Rune Poem — all 29 stanzas, one per rune of the Anglo-Saxon futhorc, recited in order from Feoh (wealth) to Ear (the grave). Each stanza names a rune and meditates on what it signifies.

⚜ Sources

  • The Anglo-Saxon Rune Poem, preserved via George Hickes's 1705 transcript of a now-destroyed Cotton Library manuscript (Otho B.x), lost in the 1731 fire.
  • English throughout is Bruce Dickins, Runic and Heroic Poems of the Old Teutonic Peoples (1915) — full text: Wikisource.
  • The poem is partly Christianised (note the references to "the Lord" / "the Creator"); several rune-names — Os, Tir, Ing — preserve older Germanic god-names beneath the surface.
ᚠ  Feoh — Wealth / Cattle
Feoh byþ frofur fira gehwylcum; sceal ðeah manna gehwylc miclun hyt dælan gif he wile for drihtne domes hleotan.
Dickins (1915)
Wealth is a comfort to all men; yet must every man bestow it freely, if he wish to gain honour in the sight of the Lord.
ᚢ  Ur — Aurochs
Ur byþ anmod ond oferhyrned, felafrecne deor, feohteþ mid hornum, mære morstapa; þæt is modig wuht.
Dickins (1915)
The aurochs is proud and has great horns; it is a very savage beast and fights with its horns; a great ranger of the moors, it is a creature of mettle.
ᚦ  Ðorn — Thorn
Ðorn byþ ðearle scearp; ðegna gehwylcum anfeng ys yfyl, ungemetum reþe manna gehwelcum, ðe him mid resteð.
Dickins (1915)
The thorn is exceedingly sharp, an evil thing for any knight to touch, uncommonly severe on all who sit among them.
ᚩ  Os — Mouth / a god
Os byþ ordfruma ælere spræce, wisdomes wraþu ond witena frofur and eorla gehwam eadnys ond tohiht.
Dickins (1915)
The mouth is the source of all language, a pillar of wisdom and a comfort to wise men, a blessing and a joy to every knight.
ᚱ  Rad — Riding
Rad byþ on recyde rinca gehwylcum sefte ond swiþhwæt, ðamðe sitteþ on ufan meare mægenheardum ofer milpaþas.
Dickins (1915)
Riding seems easy to every warrior while he is indoors and very courageous to him who traverses the high-roads on the back of a stout horse.
ᚳ  Cen — Torch
Cen byþ cwicera gehwam, cuþ on fyre, blac ond beorhtlic, byrneþ oftust ðær hi æþelingas inne restaþ.
Dickins (1915)
The torch is known to every living man by its pale, bright flame; it always burns where princes sit within.
ᚷ  Gyfu — Gift / Generosity
Gyfu gumena byþ gleng and herenys, wraþu and wyrþscype and wræcna gehwam ar and ætwist, ðe byþ oþra leas.
Dickins (1915)
Generosity brings credit and honour, which support one's dignity; it furnishes help and subsistence to all broken men who are devoid of aught else.
ᚹ  Wenne — Joy / Bliss
Wenne bruceþ, ðe can weana lyt, sares and sorge and him sylfa hæfþ blæd and blysse and eac byrga geniht.
Dickins (1915)
Bliss he enjoys who knows not suffering, sorrow nor anxiety, and has prosperity and happiness and a good enough house.
ᚻ  Hægl — Hail
Hægl byþ hwitust corna; hwyrft hit of heofones lyfte, wealcaþ hit windes scura; weorþeþ hit to wætere syððan.
Dickins (1915)
Hail is the whitest of grain; it is whirled from the vault of heaven and is tossed about by gusts of wind and then it melts into water.
ᚾ  Nyd — Need / Trouble
Nyd byþ nearu on breostan; weorþeþ hi þeah oft niþa bearnum to helpe and to hæle gehwæþre, gif hi his hlystaþ æror.
Dickins (1915)
Trouble is oppressive to the heart; yet often it proves a source of help and salvation to the children of men, to everyone who heeds it betimes.
ᛁ  Is — Ice
Is byþ ofereald, ungemetum slidor, glisnaþ glæshluttur gimmum gelicust, flor forste geworuht, fæger ansyne.
Dickins (1915)
Ice is very cold and immeasurably slippery; it glistens as clear as glass and most like to gems; it is a floor wrought by the frost, fair to look upon.
ᛄ  Ger — Year / Harvest
Ger byþ gumena hiht, ðonne God læteþ, halig heofones cyning, hrusan syllan beorhte bleda beornum ond ðearfum.
Dickins (1915)
Summer is a joy to men, when God, the holy King of Heaven, suffers the earth to bring forth shining fruits for rich and poor alike.
ᛇ  Eoh — Yew
Eoh byþ utan unsmeþe treow, heard hrusan fæst, hyrde fyres, wyrtrumun underwreþyd, wyn on eþle.
Dickins (1915)
The yew is a tree with rough bark, hard and fast in the earth, supported by its roots, a guardian of flame and a joy upon an estate.
ᛈ  Peorð — Peorth (gaming?)
Peorð byþ symble plega and hlehter wlancum [on middum], ðar wigan sittaþ on beorsele bliþe ætsomne.
Dickins (1915)
Peorth is a source of recreation and amusement to the great, where warriors sit blithely together in the banqueting-hall.
ᛉ  Eolh — Elk-sedge
Eolh-secg eard hæfþ oftust on fenne, wexeð on wature, wundaþ grimme, blode breneð beorna gehwylcne ðe him ænigne onfeng gedeþ.
Dickins (1915)
The Eolh-sedge is mostly to be found in a marsh; it grows in the water and makes a ghastly wound, covering with blood every warrior who touches it.
ᛋ  Sigel — Sun
Sigel semannum symble biþ on hihte, ðonne hi hine feriaþ ofer fisces beþ, oþ hi brimhengest bringeþ to lande.
Dickins (1915)
The sun is ever a joy in the hopes of seafarers when they journey away over the fishes' bath, until the courser of the deep bears them to land.
ᛏ  Tir — Tiw (the god / star)
Tir biþ tacna sum, healdeð trywa wel wiþ æþelingas; a biþ on færylde ofer nihta genipu, næfre swiceþ.
Dickins (1915)
Tiw is a guiding star; well does it keep faith with princes; it is ever on its course over the mists of night and never fails.
ᛒ  Beorc — Birch / Poplar
Beorc byþ bleda leas, bereþ efne swa ðeah tanas butan tudder, biþ on telgum wlitig, heah on helme hrysted fægere, geloden leafum, lyfte getenge.
Dickins (1915)
The poplar bears no fruit; yet without seed it brings forth suckers, for it is generated from its leaves. Splendid are its branches and gloriously adorned its lofty crown which reaches to the skies.
ᛖ  Eh — Horse
Eh byþ for eorlum æþelinga wyn, hors hofum wlanc, ðær him hæleþ ymb[e] welege on wicgum wrixlaþ spræce and biþ unstyllum æfre frofur.
Dickins (1915)

In Maidjan

Perhaps the oldest "song" in any Germanic language — assembled from seven of the earliest Germanic inscriptions ever found, framing a recitation of the complete Elder Futhark. The title is Proto-Germanic *maidjan "to damage, hurt" (cf. Gothic maidjan "to alter, falsify") — fitting the album's tale of a peaceful village corrupted into war.

Afhomon

The album's closing piece — and unlike the others, it is mostly modern German: an original Heilung composition, philosophical and Nietzschean in tone, rather than a historical text. Its one ancient anchor is the runic chant Gibuleubauja at the end.

A note on this entry. Most of "Afhomon" is contemporary German written by Heilung, so the renderings below are a direct translation of that German, not a sourced reading of an old text. Only the closing Gibuleubauja refrain is a historical runic formula. The German's imagery — the "will to life, to growth, to power" and "your will rules the world" — draws openly on Nietzsche's Wille zur Macht.

Futha

2019

Heilung framed Futha as the "feminine" counterpart to Ofnir: where the debut drew on rune inscriptions from weapons and armour, this album turns to Old Norse/Icelandic poetry and the magic chants of holy women, with female voices to the fore. Here in full, all nine tracks in album order.

Galgaldr

Opening track of Futha. The title fuses galdr (chanted spell). Its three parts bracket a protective charm between the fall and the rebirth of the world: the Ragnarök stanzas of Völuspá frame a genuine runic ward — the Högstena amulet.

⚜ Sources

  • Intro & outro are from Völuspá (the Poetic Edda's prophecy of the world's end and rebirth): st. 45 & the "svört verða sólskin" half-line of st. 41 (intro); st. 59 & the close of st. 64 (outro). Old Norse per the Skaldic Project; English after Bellows (1936) & Thorpe.
  • The middle galdr is the Högstena amulet (Rundata Vg 216), a lead amulet from Västergötland, Sweden bearing an Old Norse protective charm — Heilung confirm this source in their own Futha liner notes.

Like Asja, a fragment-collage rather than a translatable text — but here the fragments are real: charm-words from Migration-Period gold bracteates. Their meanings are almost entirely lost; only one word can be read with confidence, and the band built the song around it.

A note on this entry. Singer Maria Franz has said the song grows from the bracteate word landawariar — and that most of the rest cannot be translated at all. So this entry glosses the one solid anchor and clearly marks the long monosyllabic strings as untranslatable bracteate-runes blurring into ritual chant. No smooth "translation" is offered, because none honestly exists.

⚜ Sources

  • The lyrics are pieced from Migration-Period (c. 300–700 CE) gold bracteates — amulet-discs often struck from melted Roman gold, pierced to be worn for luck or protection.
  • Per Maria Franz, the one confidently readable word is landawariar / landawarijaz — "land-protector, guardian of the land." The recurring anoana is itself a bracteate charm-word (e.g. IK 131) of uncertain meaning.
  • On the difficulty: bracteate charm-words are catalogued in Mees & MacLeod, Runic Amulets and Magic Objects (2006), and the Goldbrakteaten der Völkerwanderungszeit corpus; most resist any accepted reading. From Drif (2022).

Urbani

Pure Latin — and a real historical text: the bawdy carmina that Julius Caesar’s own legionaries sang at his Gallic Triumph (46 BC), recorded by Suetonius. By ancient custom, soldiers were licensed to mock their general on his day of glory (partly to avert the evil eye). On Drif, Heilung give Rome its turn.

Rome / Latin — the soldiers’ triumph-songs

⚜ Sources

  • All three verses are quoted in Suetonius, Divus Iulius §49 & 51 — ribald marching songs from Caesar’s Gallic Triumph.
  • The "Nicomedes" verse references the rumour of young Caesar’s affair with King Nicomedes IV of Bithynia (the "Queen of Bithynia" jibe).
  • On the custom of soldiers mocking the triumphator, see the Roman triumph. From Drif (2022).
☉Latin — the bald adulterer (Suet. 51)
Urbani, servate uxores, moechum calvum adducimus. Aurum in Gallia effutuisti, hic sumpsisti mutuum.
Rendered — English
"Townsmen, guard your wives — we’re bringing home the bald adulterer. The gold you spent whoring in Gaul, you borrowed here in Rome." A jab at Caesar’s womanizing and his debts: he funded his Gallic campaigns on borrowed money and was notorious for affairs.1
☉Latin — Caesar & Nicomedes (Suet. 49)
Gallias Caesar subegit, Nicomedes Caesarem; ecce Caesar nunc triumphat qui subegit Gallias — Nicomedes non triumphat, qui subegit Caesarem.
Rendered — English
"Caesar subdued the Gauls, (but) Nicomedes (subdued) Caesar; behold, Caesar now triumphs, who subdued the Gauls — Nicomedes does not triumph, who subdued Caesar." The most cutting jibe: subigere ("to subdue") is a sexual double entendre, mocking the rumour that the young Caesar had been the lover of King Nicomedes IV of Bithynia.2
☉Latin — the Gauls in the Senate
Gallos Caesar in triumphum ducit, idem in curiam; Galli bracas deposuerunt, latum clavum sumpserunt.
Rendered — English
"Caesar leads the Gauls in triumph — and into the Senate House too; the Gauls have taken off their trousers and put on the broad (purple) stripe." Mocking Caesar for enrolling Gauls as senators: the bracae (trousers) marked them as barbarians, the latus clavus (broad purple stripe) was the mark of senatorial rank.3

¹ Suetonius, Divus Iulius 51. Caesar was famously calvus (bald) and sensitive about it — he prized the laurel wreath partly because it hid the baldness. His Gallic Wars were hugely expensive and debt-financed.

² Suetonius, Divus Iulius 49. The Nicomedes rumour (c. 80 BC) dogged Caesar all his life — Cicero and others used the "Queen of Bithynia" taunt; Caesar denied it under oath. That his own soldiers sang it at his triumph shows the remarkable licence Roman troops had to mock their commander.

³ A political jibe rather than a sexual one: Caesar’s enfranchisement of Gauls and his expansion of the Senate scandalised conservatives. The contrast of bracae (barbarian trousers) with the latus clavus (senatorial stripe) captures the perceived absurdity. Together the three songs are a vivid survival of actual Roman soldiers’ voices — obscene, political, and affectionate all at once.

Keltentrauer

"Celtic mourning / grief." A long original-German narrative poem — the Gaulish counterpart to Schlammschlacht: a Celtic ("Keltui") host meets the Roman legions in pitched battle and is destroyed. The body is Heilung’s own German (translated here); its historical and cultural anchors — the carnyx, the single-combat challenge, and the cry Vae victis — are verified below.

Gaul / Celtic Rome (the legions & the Latin cry)
A note on this entry. The German is Heilung’s own composition, so the renderings are translation, not philology. ⚜ Sources

  • The carnyx: the Celtic Iron-Age war-horn (c. 200 BC–200 AD), a tall bronze trumpet with a boar’s-head bell, used to incite warriors and unnerve foes (Polybius).

Nesso

A genuine medieval healing charm: the Old Saxon Contra vermem ("against the worm"), c. 900–1000 AD. It talks a disease step by step out of the body — marrow, bone, flesh, skin — and finally into an arrow, to be cast away. Back to the codex’s native Germanic ground after Drif’s journeys abroad.

Nikkal

The deepest the codex reaches into antiquity: the Hurrian Hymn to Nikkal ("h.6"), inscribed in cuneiform at Ugarit (modern Ras Shamra, Syria) around 1400 BCE — the oldest substantially complete piece of notated music known to exist, over 3,200 years old. A devotional hymn to a Bronze-Age moon-goddess of the orchards.

Ugarit / Hurrian — Late Bronze Age Syria
A note on this entry. The Hurrian lyrics survive on a broken, water-damaged tablet and are only partly understood; no line-by-line translation is secure. A prayer to Nikkal. The tablet’s damage and the limits of Hurrian leave the lyrics only partly legible, but the surviving sense is a fertility-invocation: an offering laid before the goddess so that she may grant increase — in the fuller readings, that barren women may be made fruitful and "in her heart" receive the seed she blesses. The repeated ḫanuku / ḫanuta and the goddess-name nikala anchor the chant; a literal line-by-line meaning is not recoverable.1

¹ Heilung sing from the reconstructed transliteration of the tablet, not from a settled translation — four or more scholarly teams have proposed differing readings of both the words and the melody, and Dr Richard Dumbrill’s well-known musical reconstruction is itself called "highly conjectural." What is firm: the hymn is c. 3,400 years old, written in cuneiform at Ugarit, addressed to the orchard-and-moon goddess Nikkal, and is the earliest notated music humanity has recovered. For Drif — an album reaching toward "all great ancient civilizations" — it is the furthest journey of all, from the Norse North to Late Bronze-Age Syria, and a fitting near-close: the oldest song in the world, sung again.

Marduk

The codex’s final chapter: the Fifty Names of Marduk from the Enūma Eliš, the Babylonian epic of creation. Having slain the chaos-dragon Tiamat and built the ordered world, Marduk is proclaimed king of the gods, who bestow on him fifty ceremonial names — each a title of his power. The song recites them, from Marduk to the great star Nēberu.

"You have killed the demon." Heilung’s own line, marking the heart of the myth: Marduk’s slaying of Tiamat, the primordial chaos from whose body he forms the heavens and the earth. The deed that earns him the fifty names.2

¹ In the Enūma Eliš, after Marduk defeats Tiamat and orders the cosmos, the gods assemble and grant him fifty names — some absorbed from older deities he supersedes, others epithets of his powers (storm, fertility, justice, healing, kingship).
